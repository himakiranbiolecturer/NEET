13.1 · Variety at many scales

Levels of biodiversity

Biodiversity is the combined diversity across biological organisation. For NCERT and NEET, focus on three major levels.

Within a species

Genetic diversity

Variation in genes or traits among populations or varieties of the same species. NCERT examples include variation in Rauwolfia, rice strains and mango varieties.

Among species

Species diversity

Variety and relative abundance of species in a region; the Western Ghats show greater amphibian diversity than the Eastern Ghats.

Among habitats

Ecological diversity

Variety of ecosystems such as deserts, forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows.

13.1.2 · Predictable trends

Patterns of biodiversity

1. Latitudinal gradient

Species diversity generally decreases from the equator toward the poles. Tropical regions have had longer undisturbed evolutionary time, more constant environments that favour niche specialisation, and greater solar energy supporting productivity.

2. Species–area relationship

Alexander von Humboldt observed that species richness increases with explored area, up to a limit. The arithmetic plot is a rectangular hyperbola; log transformation produces a straight line.

S = C AZ   ⇄   log S = log C + Z log A

Ordinary regions

Z commonly lies between 0.1 and 0.2, across many taxa and regions.

Very large areas

Across continents, Z is steeper: 0.6 to 1.2. For tropical frugivorous birds and mammals, NCERT gives Z = 1.15.

13.1.3 · Diversity and stability

Why species richness matters

Stable productivity

A stable community shows relatively little year-to-year variation in productivity.

Resistance & resilience

It can resist or recover from occasional natural or human-made disturbances.

Invasion resistance

It is better able to resist establishment by alien species.

David Tilman's long-term experimentsPlots with more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass, and increased diversity contributed to higher productivity.

Rivet-popper hypothesis

Paul Ehrlich compared species to rivets holding an aircraft together. Losing a few may not immediately stop ecosystem function, but continuing losses weaken the system; losing a key functional species can be especially serious.

Correct interpretationThe analogy does not say that every species loss has an identical effect. It emphasises cumulative loss and the special importance of key species.
13.1.4 · Human-driven decline

Loss of biodiversity

Current extinction rates cited in NCERT are estimated to be 100–1,000 times faster than pre-human rates. Biodiversity loss can reduce plant production, lower resistance to environmental disturbance and increase variability in ecosystem processes.

13.2.1 · Values of living diversity

Why should we conserve biodiversity?

Direct value

Narrowly utilitarian

Food, fibre, firewood, construction material, industrial products, medicines and bioprospecting.

Ecosystem value

Broadly utilitarian

Pollination, oxygen production, climate regulation, nutrient cycling and aesthetic or cultural benefits.

Intrinsic value

Ethical

Every species has value independent of immediate economic usefulness; biodiversity is a legacy for future generations.

Classification trapA medicinal plant with market value is narrowly utilitarian. Pollination as an ecosystem service is broadly utilitarian. A species' right to exist is ethical.
13.2.2 · Protection strategies

How do we conserve biodiversity?

ApproachWhat is protected?Examples
In situ — on siteSpecies within their natural ecosystem; many interacting species protected togetherBiosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biodiversity hotspots, sacred groves
Ex situ — off siteThreatened organisms or germplasm outside the natural habitat under specialised careZoos, botanical gardens, safari parks, seed banks, cryopreservation, tissue culture, in-vitro fertilisation

Biodiversity hotspots

Hotspots combine very high species richness and endemism with rapid habitat loss. The chapter identifies 34 global hotspots and lists three covering India's exceptionally diverse regions: Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and Himalaya.

NCERT-edition noteProtected-area and hotspot totals in the textbook are chapter-specific figures. For NEET, distinguish these cited facts from changing present-day inventories.

Sacred groves

Community traditions have protected forest patches in the Khasi and Jaintia Hills, Aravalli Hills, Western Ghats regions of Karnataka and Maharashtra, and the Sarguja–Chanda–Bastar region cited in the chapter.

Core vocabulary

Glossary

Biodiversity Combined biological variety at genetic, species and ecological levels.
Endemism Restriction of a species to a particular geographical region.
Hotspot Region of high richness and endemism experiencing serious habitat loss.
Bioprospecting Exploration of biodiversity for useful genetic, molecular or commercial products.
In situ conservation Protection within the natural habitat.
Ex situ conservation Protection outside the natural habitat.
Co-extinction Loss of one species following extinction of an obligatorily associated species.
Species richness Number of species present in a defined area.
