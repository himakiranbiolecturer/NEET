01 · Origin

From chemical evolution to cellular life

Evolutionary biology explains the history and diversification of life; the origin of life asks how the first living systems arose.

Early Earth
reducing conditions
→
Simple inorganic molecules
→
Organic molecules
→
Self-replicating systems → cells
Oparin–Haldane

Proposed chemical evolution: organic compounds could form from non-living molecules under early-Earth conditions.

Miller experiment

Electrical sparks through a methane–ammonia–hydrogen–water system produced amino acids and other organic compounds.

First organisms

The earliest life was simple and anaerobic. Photosynthesis later released oxygen, changing atmosphere and metabolism.

NEET distinction

Miller demonstrated abiotic synthesis of organic molecules—not the creation of life itself.

02 · Evidence

Different records tell the same story

Homologous structures

Same basic plan and origin, different functions: vertebrate forelimbs. They indicate common ancestry and divergent evolution.

Analogous structures

Different origin, similar function: wings of birds and butterflies. They reflect convergent evolution.

Vestigial structures

Reduced remnants inherited from ancestors; they support descent with modification.

Connecting links

Organisms or fossils with features of two groups help reconstruct evolutionary transitions.

03 · Ideas

How explanations changed

IdeaCentral claimNEET evaluation
LamarckismUse/disuse and inheritance of acquired characters drive change.Historically important, but acquired somatic changes are generally not inherited.
DarwinismHeritable variations plus struggle for existence lead to differential reproductive success.Natural selection is central, though Darwin lacked a genetic mechanism.
Mutation theoryHugo de Vries emphasised sudden, discontinuous changes.Mutation creates variation; evolution also depends on recombination, selection, drift and gene flow.
Modern synthesisPopulation genetics integrates Mendelian inheritance with Darwinian selection.Evolution is change in allele frequencies over generations.
Fitness is reproductive success

In evolution, “fit” means leaving more viable, fertile offspring in a particular environment—not necessarily being strongest or longest-lived.

04 · Natural selection

Selection acts on phenotypes; populations evolve

Heritable variation
→
More offspring than resources support
→
Differential survival & reproduction
→
Allele-frequency change
Industrial melanism

Environmental change altered which moth colour was better camouflaged. The example demonstrates selection on existing variation.

Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotics do not direct useful mutations. Resistant variants already present or newly arising leave more descendants during exposure.

Pesticide resistance

Repeated pesticide use increases the frequency of resistance alleles by differential survival.

Individual versus population

Individuals are selected; allele frequencies change in populations. An individual does not evolve during its lifetime.

05 · Diversification

Adaptive radiation and convergent outcomes

Adaptive radiation

Multiple species evolve from a common ancestor in one geographical area, each adapted to a different ecological niche.

Darwin’s finches

An ancestral finch lineage diversified on the Galápagos; beak forms became suited to different foods.

Australian marsupials

One ancestral stock diversified into many forms occupying distinct habitats and roles.

Divergent evolution

Common ancestry produces different structures or species: homologous organs and adaptive radiation.

Convergent evolution

Unrelated lineages face similar pressures and independently evolve similar adaptations: analogous organs.

06 · Population genetics

Five forces can alter allele frequencies

Mutation

Creates new alleles and is the ultimate source of novel genetic variation.

Recombination

Reshuffles existing alleles through crossing over, independent assortment and fertilisation.

Gene flow

Migration moves alleles between populations and can reduce differences between them.

Genetic drift

Random frequency change, strongest in small populations. Founder and bottleneck effects are special cases.

Natural selection

Non-random differences in reproductive success increase advantageous heritable variants in a given environment.

Isolation

Reduced gene flow permits populations to diverge; reproductive isolation is crucial for speciation.

07 · Hardy–Weinberg lab

Test the genetic equilibrium

For two alleles A and a: p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Move q to see expected genotype frequencies under equilibrium.

Assumes a very large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection.

Common recessive-phenotype shortcut

If the recessive phenotype frequency is given as q², first take its square root to find q; then p = 1 − q and carriers = 2pq.

08 · Selection patterns

Track one generation of viability selection

After selection

Stabilising

Intermediate phenotypes are favoured; variation around the mean decreases.

Directional

One extreme is favoured; the population mean shifts toward that extreme.

Disruptive

Both extremes are favoured over intermediates; variation may become bimodal.

09 · History of life

A compressed biological timeline

~3.8 billion years ago
Very early cellular life appeared in aquatic environments.
Later Precambrian
Photosynthetic organisms raised atmospheric oxygen; eukaryotic and multicellular life emerged.
Palaeozoic era
Major diversification of marine life; fishes, land plants, amphibians and early reptiles appeared.
Mesozoic era
Reptiles dominated; birds and early mammals evolved; flowering plants expanded later.
Cenozoic era
Mammals and birds diversified extensively; primate and human lineages evolved.
Timeline caution

Use geological sequence and relative order for NEET. Exact dates may vary with updated evidence; follow the current NCERT wording for exam-specific numbers.

10 · Human evolution

A branching history—not a ladder

Early hominoids

Dryopithecus was more ape-like; Ramapithecus was described in older accounts as more human-like.

Australopithecines

Bipedal African hominins with a mixture of ape-like and human-like features.

Homo habilis → H. erectus

Tool use became clearer; brain size increased and H. erectus spread beyond Africa.

Homo sapiens

Modern humans arose in Africa and dispersed widely, developing complex language, symbolism and culture.

Shared primate ancestor
→

→
Genus Homo
→
Homo sapiens
Do not draw a straight chimp-to-human line

Humans and living apes share ancestors. Present-day apes are evolutionary relatives, not our direct ancestors.

12 · Rapid glossary

Last-minute definitions

Evolution

Heritable change in populations across generations, often measured as allele-frequency change.

Fitness

Relative reproductive contribution of a genotype or phenotype to future generations.

Natural selection

Non-random differential reproduction associated with heritable phenotypic differences.

Genetic drift

Random change in allele frequencies, especially strong in small populations.

Gene flow

Transfer of alleles between populations through migration and reproduction.

Adaptive radiation

Diversification of a common ancestral lineage into species adapted to different niches.

Homology

Similarity due to common ancestry, even when functions differ.

Speciation

Evolutionary formation of new species, usually involving reproductive isolation.
